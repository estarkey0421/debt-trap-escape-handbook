# The Debt Trap Escape Handbook
### A state-by-state guide to your rights against unlicensed, tribal, and other high-interest predatory lenders
**Second Edition (v1.4) — July 2026**

> **READ THIS FIRST.** This is general legal information, not legal advice. Lending law changes every year and varies by state; the state table below is a map, not gospel — **always run the Verification Protocol before you stop paying anything.** And a bright line: this handbook exists to help people escape debts the law already says they do not owe. **Taking out a loan you never intend to repay is fraud** — false statements on a loan application are a crime with a paper trail, and nothing here protects it. If that's your plan, this document is not for you.

---

## Quick Start — If You Read Nothing Else

You are being drained by a loan you probably don't legally owe, and you are about three hours of work away from making it stop. Six steps, today, in this order:

1. **Find your state in the Part 4 table.** Green (Tier 1)? The loan is likely VOID — legally nonexistent. Yellow (Tier 2)? You likely owe the principal at a lawful rate and not a penny more. Orange or red? Skip to the Universal Playbook (Part 2); different tools, still real ones.
2. **Check the law yourself (15 minutes).** Your state's row shows a code in the "Key law" column — letters and numbers like RCW 31.04.035. That code is the law's name. Type it into Google with the word "law" and click the result that ends in **.gov**. You're looking for one word: **"void"** (or "uncollectible" or "unenforceable"). See it? The table was right. Confused at any point? Skip the reading — call the regulator listed in your state's row and ask two questions: *"Is [lender name] licensed in this state?"* and *"What does the law say about loans made by unlicensed lenders?"* They answer these for free, every day.
3. **Call your bank.** Use the number on the back of your debit card and say exactly: *"I need a stop payment on all ACH debits and charges from [lender name], and I am revoking my authorization for them to debit my account. Please block all future debits."* Pay the stop-payment fee if there is one — it's cheaper than one more loan payment. This stops the bleeding within days.
4. **Send the letter.** The fill-in-the-blank letter is in **Appendix A** at the back of this handbook. (If you also have the State Action Kits companion, use your state's pre-filled version instead — it's stronger.) Fill in every [BRACKET], sign it, and send it two ways the same day: certified mail with return receipt (about $9 at any post office — keep the receipt forever) and email to the lender's support address.
5. **File the four complaints.** (a) Your state regulator — go to the website in your state's row and click "File a Complaint"; (b) your state Attorney General — Google "[your state] attorney general consumer complaint"; (c) CFPB — consumerfinance.gov/complaint or 1-855-411-2372; (d) FTC — ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Free, under an hour total, and it builds the record that protects you and everyone who comes after you.
6. **Read Part 6.** The phone is about to start ringing. Part 6 tells you exactly what they'll say, why every threat is a bluff — and the one piece of mail you must never, ever ignore.

## Part 1 — How the Trap Works

A predatory small-dollar loan is engineered so that repayment barely touches the principal. A typical real example: $500 borrowed at 531% APR, repaid in 26 biweekly payments of $107.63 — **$2,800 total**. Payments are applied to fees first, then interest, then principal, so a borrower can pay back three times what they borrowed and still owe nearly the full balance. When payments become impossible, the lender offers to "help" — with a new loan. That's not a bug: regulators and courts have documented that high-rate lenders profit even when borrowers default.

The lenders charging these rates almost never hold a state license, because **no state license permits these rates.** They operate through evasion structures instead: **tribal lending** (the loan claims to be governed only by tribal law, often with the real economics flowing to outside non-tribal investors — the "rent-a-tribe" model), **rent-a-bank**, and anonymous online lenders. Courts and attorneys general have spent fifteen years dismantling these structures: Think Finance and CashCall/Western Sky were shut down, operators Scott Tucker and Charles Hallinan went to federal prison, and class settlements — including the ~491,000-borrower settlement involving Big Picture Loans — have cancelled hundreds of millions in debt.

**The single most important fact in this handbook:** in a large group of states, a loan made by an unlicensed lender, or above the state rate cap, is not merely "illegal" — it is **void**. Void means the debt does not legally exist. The lender cannot enforce it in court, and in several states you can claw back everything already paid. Legislatures wrote these statutes as a deterrent, on purpose. A deterrent nobody knows about deters nothing. Knowing about it is the whole game.

## Part 2 — The Universal Playbook (works in every state)

These steps rest on federal law and on the loan contracts themselves. Do them in order.

1. **Get your documents.** Download your loan agreement and pull your full payment history from your bank. Note the APR, total of payments, the lender's exact legal name, and any "license" it claims. A license issued by the lender's own tribe is not a state license.
2. **Check the license.** Search the lender at **nmlsconsumeraccess.org** and on your state regulator's "verify a license" page (table below). Many regulators have published consumer alerts naming your exact lender as unlicensed. Screenshot everything.
3. **Find your state's tier** in the table and **run the Verification Protocol** (Part 5).
4. **Cut off the auto-debits — bank first, lender second.** Under the Electronic Fund Transfer Act / Regulation E (12 C.F.R. § 1005.10(c)) you may revoke ACH authorization at any time and your bank must honor a stop-payment order. Call the bank and block all debits from the lender; then send the lender written revocation (their own contract almost always has a revocation clause — use it). Never rely on the lender to stop debiting.
5. **Send a written dispute / cease-collection letter** by certified mail (return receipt) plus email: cite your state statute from the table, demand collection cease, revoke consent to calls/texts (TCPA, 47 U.S.C. § 227), and demand no credit reporting of the disputed debt.
6. **File all four complaints, every time:** (a) your state financial regulator; (b) your state Attorney General; (c) the **CFPB** — consumerfinance.gov/complaint or 1-855-411-2372 (the company must respond in writing in ~15 days); (d) the **FTC** — ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Free, fast, and they build the enforcement record that shuts these lenders down for everyone.
7. **Guard your credit.** Check all three bureaus free at annualcreditreport.com. If the void debt appears, dispute it in writing (FCRA, 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2). Note: most tribal/unlicensed lenders don't report to the bureaus at all — the "we'll ruin your credit" threat is usually a bluff.
8. **Know the collector rules.** Third-party collectors are bound by the FDCPA (15 U.S.C. § 1692): demand written validation within 30 days. Collecting a debt that's void under state law — or threatening a suit that can't legally be filed — violates the FDCPA. Many collectors also need their own state license.
9. **Military? You have a nuclear option.** The Military Lending Act (10 U.S.C. § 987) caps loans to active-duty members and dependents at 36% MAPR nationwide; loans above it are **void from inception**. Contact your installation JAG office.
10. **Don't fall for the sequel.** "Settlement" and "consolidation" companies that cold-call you are frequently a second scam feeding on the first. Talk to a nonprofit (Part 7) before paying anyone.

## Part 3 — The Tribal Lender Reality Check

Tribal lenders lead with two claims: only tribal law governs the loan, and sovereign immunity makes them untouchable. Both are narrower than they sound. Immunity makes it hard to **sue the tribal entity** — but even that has limits (the Fourth Circuit's *Williams v. Big Picture Loans* found immunity, yet the enterprise still ended in a ~half-million-borrower settlement, and its outside financier took a **$43.4 million judgment**, because immunity never protects the non-tribal partners and investors who run these schemes).

What immunity does **NOT** do: it does not make a void loan valid. Choice-of-law clauses picking tribal law over your state's protections are routinely held unenforceable as against public policy (*Gingras v. Think Finance*; *MacDonald v. CashCall*; *Hengle v. Treppa*). It does not let the lender sue you in your state and win — your state's court applies your state's void-loan statute. And in practice tribal lenders essentially **never sue borrowers**, because filing suit would expose the whole structure to a court and to counterclaims.

**The practical asymmetry:** as a *defense*, a void-loan statute is close to bulletproof — stop paying, block the debits, and the lender's only real move is angry letters. As *offense* — clawing back what you paid — immunity makes things slower, usually requiring the non-tribal participants, an AG action, or a class case. **Escaping the debt is the reliable win; refunds are the bonus round.**

## Part 5 — The Verification Protocol (do this BEFORE you stop paying)

Fifteen minutes of checking protects you from the one real risk in this handbook: acting on a stale entry. Laws change every year — Washington's void provision is from 2024, Minnesota's cap from 2023, and states occasionally move backward too. You do not need to be a lawyer to do this. Follow the steps mechanically:

1. **Get the law's name from the table.** In your state's row in Part 4, look at the "Key law" column. You'll see a code made of letters and numbers — for example, RCW 31.04.035 (that's Washington's) or 815 ILCS 123 (that's Illinois's). That code IS the name of the law. Copy it down exactly, symbols and all.
2. **Read the one sentence that matters.** Type the code into Google, add the word "law", and press enter. In the results, click the link that ends in **.gov** — that's your state legislature's official copy (ignore ad results and law-firm blogs). You don't need to understand the whole page. You're hunting for a single sentence about what happens to a loan made without a license or above the rate cap, and the words you want to see are **"void," "uncollectible," "unenforceable,"** or **"forfeits interest."** Found one of those words? The table was right — keep going.
3. **Confirm the lender has no license.** Go to **nmlsconsumeraccess.org** and type the lender's name in the search box — this is the national database of licensed lenders. Then go to your state regulator's website (it's in your state's row) and find the page called "Verify a License" or "License Lookup" and search there too. No results in either place means unlicensed. While you're on the regulator's site, also search the lender's name — many regulators have published warnings naming these exact companies. Screenshot everything.
4. **Confirm your loan fits.** Three quick checks: the date you took the loan is *after* the law's effective date (some protections only cover new loans); the loan amount is within any dollar limit the law mentions; and you were physically in your state when you applied. All three yes? You're covered.
5. **Stuck or unsure at ANY step? Don't guess — call.** Every state regulator answers consumer questions for free. Call the number on the Contact page of the website in your state's row and ask the two questions: *"Is [lender name] licensed to lend in this state?"* and *"What does state law say about loans made by unlicensed lenders?"* They can't give legal advice, but they answer both of those every single day. And ten minutes with a free legal aid attorney (lawhelp.org) beats months of guessing.

## Part 6 — After You Send the Letter: The Fear Campaign, the Bluffs, and the One Real Danger

The law did its job the day your letter arrived. What happens next is theater, and it follows a script so predictable you can read it in advance. Expect the calls to **spike** in the first two weeks (urgency is their only remaining tool), then thin out. Somewhere in the following months, your account may be sold to a debt buyer or handed to a collector, who restarts the script from the top. Each handoff makes your position stronger, not weaker: buyers of void debt inherit its voidness, and third-party collectors are bound by the federal FDCPA in ways the original lender wasn't. Here is the bluff decoder — what they say, and what is actually true:

- **"We're filing a lawsuit this week."** Tribal and unlicensed lenders almost never sue, because a courtroom is the one place their whole structure gets examined — the missing license, the void-loan statute, and your counterclaims with attorney's fees attached. And a collector threatening a suit it doesn't intend to file is itself violating the FDCPA (15 U.S.C. § 1692e(5)). Log the call; it's evidence.
- **"You'll be arrested" / "this is criminal fraud."** There is no debtors' prison in the United States. Failure to pay a consumer debt is never a crime, police do not call about loans, and anyone who says otherwise is lying to you — and if they're a third-party collector, breaking federal law while doing it.
- **"A process server will be at your workplace tomorrow."** Real service of process does not come with a panic-inducing warning call offering to make it all go away for a payment today. This exact line is the signature of the phantom-debt script.
- **"We'll garnish your wages / freeze your account / take your car."** Garnishment requires suing you, winning, and getting a court judgment first. No judgment, no garnishment. Ever.
- **"We'll ruin your credit."** Most of these lenders never report to the major bureaus at all, and knowingly reporting a disputed void debt exposes them under the FCRA. Check annualcreditreport.com; dispute anything that appears, attaching your letter.
- **"This is your final notice before escalation to our legal department."** You will receive several final notices. It's a rotation, not an escalation.

**The phantom-debt scammers.** After a default, loan-application data gets sold and resold, and eventually strangers call who know your Social Security number and quote your old loan back to you while threatening arrest. Three rules end every one of these calls: (1) a legitimate collector must send written validation of the debt and must honor your demand for it — say "send validation in writing" and hang up; under the FDCPA you have 30 days from their validation notice to dispute, and collection must pause until they respond; (2) anyone demanding payment by gift card, wire transfer, cryptocurrency, or a payment app is a scammer, one hundred percent of the time, no exceptions; (3) never confirm your SSN, date of birth, or bank details to an inbound caller — ever. Report the call at ReportFraud.ftc.gov; phantom collection is an active FTC enforcement priority.

**What never to say or do on these calls.** Do not promise a payment. Do not make a "good-faith" partial payment on a disputed or void debt — in some states a payment restarts the statute of limitations, and it undercuts the dispute you built. Do not give a new bank account or card number. Do not accept a "settlement" on a Tier 1 void loan; you would be paying real money on a legally nonexistent debt. If any offer interests you, require it in writing first, and run it past legal aid before signing anything.

**If a debit sneaks through after your revocation.** Your bank is your backstop: under Regulation E, an electronic debit initiated after you've revoked authorization is an *unauthorized transfer*. Notify the bank in writing within 60 days of the statement showing it, attach your revocation letter, and demand an error investigation — the bank generally must resolve it within 10 business days or issue provisional credit while it investigates. This is a right against your *bank*, so it works even when the lender ignores you completely.

**⚠️ THE ONE REAL DANGER: a genuine court summons.** Everything in this handbook rests on one discipline — "void" is an affirmative defense, a shield you must *show up* to raise. The only reliable way a dead debt becomes a live wage garnishment is a **default judgment**, entered because someone believed "they can't sue me" and ignored real court papers. So: real papers carry a court caption, a case number, and a courthouse address — verify by calling the court clerk using the phone number on the court's own .gov website, never a number printed on the papers themselves. If it's fake, you've caught a scammer; report it. If it's real: respond before the deadline (typically 20–30 days), raise the void-loan statute from your State Action Kit as your defense, attach your letter and certified-mail receipt, and call legal aid the same day. These are the cases consumer attorneys take eagerly — fee-shifting means the lender pays your lawyer — and lenders routinely dismiss rather than let a court rule their loan void on the record. Never, under any circumstances, ignore genuine court papers.

**What winning looks like.** An anticlimax. The calls spike, then thin. The debits stop. Nothing lands on your credit. Months later you notice the last contact was a form letter, and the loan hasn't ended with a certificate or an apology — it has simply ceased to exist in anyone's ledger except your folder of receipts. Keep that folder for seven years: zombie debt buyers occasionally surface years later, and one reply enclosing your dispute letter and certified-mail receipt kills the resurrection on contact. That silence is the whole victory. It's also the first payday-free paycheck you've had in a long time — spend it on yourself.

## Part 7 — Getting Your Money Back, and Getting Help

**Refund routes, in rough order of effort:** (1) **Demand it** in your cease-collection letter — in void states, payments beyond principal (in Virginia, ALL payments) are legally recoverable, and some lenders refund to make complaints disappear. (2) The **CFPB complaint** forces a written company response and sometimes shakes out a refund. (3) **Your state AG** — AGs have repeatedly forced tribal/online lenders to cancel debts and pay restitution statewide (Vermont, West Virginia, Pennsylvania, Virginia, and others). (4) A **private UDAP lawsuit** — most states award attorney's fees and many award double/treble damages, which is why consumer attorneys often take these free. (5) **Class actions** — search your lender's name + "class action settlement"; you may already be owed money.

**Free help directory**
- Legal aid by zip code: **lawhelp.org** and **lsc.gov/find-legal-aid**
- Consumer attorneys (fee-shifted, often free to you): **consumeradvocates.org/find-an-attorney** (NACA)
- Nonprofit credit counseling: **nfcc.org**, 1-800-388-2227
- The law in plain English: **nclc.org** (their 50-state surveys are the backbone of the table above)
- Servicemembers: installation JAG office; **Military OneSource** 1-800-342-9647
- Federal portals: **consumerfinance.gov/complaint** (CFPB, 1-855-411-2372) · **ReportFraud.ftc.gov** (FTC, 1-877-382-4357)

## Appendix A — The Letter and the Scripts

*Everything below is self-contained — you can execute the whole plan with just this handbook. If you also have the State Action Kits companion, use your state's pre-filled letter instead of the universal one below; it has your exact statute language already written in.*

**The bank call script.** Call the number on the back of your debit card and say exactly:

> "I need a stop payment on all ACH debits and any other charges from a company called [LENDER NAME], and I am revoking my authorization for them to debit my account. Please block all future debits from them."

If asked why: *"The loan is unlawful under state law and I have sent the company a written revocation."* Ask about the stop-payment fee (usually $25–35 — pay it). Write down the date, the time, and the name of the person you spoke to.

**The regulator call script.** Call the number on the Contact page of your state regulator's website (it's in your state's row in Part 4) and ask:

> "Is [LENDER NAME] licensed to lend in this state? And what does state law say about loans made by unlicensed lenders?"

**The universal letter.** Fill in every [BRACKET]. For [KEY LAW], copy the code from the "Key law" column of your state's row in Part 4. For [WHAT THE LAW SAYS], copy the consequence from that same row — for example, "a non-mortgage loan made by an unlicensed lender is null, void, uncollectable, and unenforceable." Sign it, then send it two ways the same day: certified mail with return receipt, and email.
