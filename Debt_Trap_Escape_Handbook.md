# The Debt Trap Escape Handbook
### A state-by-state guide to your rights against unlicensed, tribal, and other high-interest predatory lenders
**Second Edition (v1.6) — August 2026**

> **This guide is free, sells nothing, and collects nothing.** It will never ask for your Social Security number, your bank login, or a payment. Every link goes to a government (.gov) or nonprofit site. If anyone contacts you claiming to represent this guide and asks for money or personal information, they are not us.
>
> **Everything in here can be done for $0.** Some steps have a paid version and a free version. The free version is always given. Do not spend money you need for rent, food, or utilities on any step in this handbook.
>
> **If your power is about to be shut off, or you can't buy food this week, deal with that first.** Dial **211** or go to **211.org** — free, confidential, nationwide, connects you to local rent, utility, and food assistance. Then come back.

> **READ THIS FIRST.** This is general legal information, not legal advice. Lending law changes every year and varies by state; the state table below is a map, not gospel — **always run the Verification Protocol before you stop paying anything.** And a bright line: this handbook exists to help people escape debts the law already says they do not owe. **Taking out a loan you never intend to repay is fraud** — false statements on a loan application are a crime with a paper trail, and nothing here protects it. If that's your plan, this document is not for you.

## Quick Start — If You Read Nothing Else

You are being drained by a loan you probably don't legally owe, and you are about three hours of work away from making it stop. Six steps, today, in this order. (More than one loan? That is the normal shape of this trap — the same six steps work on each one, and Part 8 explains how to run them in parallel.)

1. **Find your state in the Part 4 table.** Green (Tier 1)? The loan is likely VOID — legally nonexistent. Yellow (Tier 2)? You likely owe the principal at a lawful rate and not a penny more. **Orange or red (Tier 3 or 4)? Stop here and go to Part 2A instead.** The void-loan letter in this handbook is not for you and sending it could make things worse — but five other tools still are, and Part 2A lists them.
2. **Check the law yourself (15 minutes).** Your state's row shows a code in the "Key law" column — letters and numbers like RCW 31.04.035. That code is the law's name. Type it into Google with the word "law" and click the result that ends in **.gov**. You're looking for one word: **"void"** (or "uncollectible" or "unenforceable"). See it? The table was right. Confused at any point? Skip the reading — call the regulator listed in your state's row and ask two questions: *"Is [lender name] licensed in this state?"* and *"What does the law say about loans made by unlicensed lenders?"* They answer these for free, every day.
3. **Call your bank — and ask for the free version.** There are two different things you can ask for, and they are not priced the same. **Revoking your ACH authorization is your right and should cost nothing.** A formal **stop-payment order** is a separate service most banks charge $25–$35 for. Ask for the revocation first: *"I am revoking my authorization for [lender name] to debit my account. Please block all future debits from them. I am not asking for a paid stop-payment order unless that is the only way to block them."* **Timing matters:** under federal rules the bank generally needs your notice **at least three business days before** the next scheduled debit, and may ask you to confirm in writing within 14 days — so if a payment is due in the next day or two, it may still go through. Ask the bank directly: *"Will this stop the payment scheduled on [date]?"* If it won't, ask what will. Write down the date, time, and the name of the person you spoke to.
4. **Send the letter — Tier 1 and Tier 2 only.** The fill-in-the-blank letter is in **Appendix A**. (If you have the State Action Kits companion, use your state's pre-filled version — it's stronger.) Fill in every [BRACKET] and sign it. **The free way to send it:** email it to the lender's support address and screenshot the sent message, then mail a printed copy by ordinary first-class stamp. **The stronger way, if you have the money:** certified mail with return receipt, about $9. Certified mail is better proof, but a saved sent-email plus a mailed copy is real evidence and costs almost nothing. Do not skip sending the letter because you can't afford certified mail.
5. **File the four complaints.** (a) Your state regulator — go to the website in your state's row and click "File a Complaint"; (b) your state Attorney General — Google "[your state] attorney general consumer complaint"; (c) CFPB — consumerfinance.gov/complaint or 1-855-411-2372; (d) FTC — ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Free, under an hour total, and it builds the record that protects you and everyone who comes after you.
6. **Read Part 6.** The phone is about to start ringing. Part 6 tells you exactly what they'll say, why every threat is a bluff — and the one piece of mail you must never, ever ignore.

## Part 1 — How the Trap Works

A predatory small-dollar loan is engineered so that repayment barely touches the principal. A typical real example: $500 borrowed at 531% APR, repaid in 26 biweekly payments of $107.63 — **$2,800 total**. Payments are applied to fees first, then interest, then principal, so a borrower can pay back three times what they borrowed and still owe nearly the full balance. When payments become impossible, the lender offers to "help" — with a new loan. That's not a bug: regulators and courts have documented that high-rate lenders profit even when borrowers default.

The lenders charging these rates almost never hold a state license, because **no state license permits these rates.** They operate through evasion structures instead: **tribal lending** (the loan claims to be governed only by tribal law, often with the real economics flowing to outside non-tribal investors — the "rent-a-tribe" model), **rent-a-bank**, and anonymous online lenders. Courts and attorneys general have spent fifteen years dismantling these structures: Think Finance and CashCall/Western Sky were shut down, operators Scott Tucker and Charles Hallinan went to federal prison, and class settlements — including the ~491,000-borrower settlement involving Big Picture Loans — have cancelled hundreds of millions in debt.

**The single most important fact in this handbook:** in a large group of states, a loan made by an unlicensed lender, or above the state rate cap, is not merely "illegal" — it is **void**. Void means the debt does not legally exist. The lender cannot enforce it in court, and in several states you can claw back everything already paid. Legislatures wrote these statutes as a deterrent, on purpose. A deterrent nobody knows about deters nothing. Knowing about it is the whole game.

## Part 2 — The Universal Playbook (works in every state)

These steps rest on federal law and on the loan contracts themselves. Do them in order.

1. **Get your documents.** Download your loan agreement and pull your full payment history from your bank. Note the APR, total of payments, the lender's exact legal name, and any "license" it claims. A license issued by the lender's own tribe is not a state license.
2. **Check the license.** Search the lender at **nmlsconsumeraccess.org** and on your state regulator's "verify a license" page (table below). Many regulators have published consumer alerts naming your exact lender as unlicensed. Screenshot everything.
3. **Find your state's tier** in the table and **run the Verification Protocol** (Part 5).
4. **Cut off the auto-debits — bank first, lender second.** Under the Electronic Fund Transfer Act and Regulation E (12 C.F.R. § 1005.10(c)) you may revoke your authorization for preauthorized electronic transfers. Three details the fine print actually contains, and that matter to you: the bank generally needs your order **at least three business days before** the scheduled transfer; the bank **may require written confirmation within 14 days** of an oral order, so send that confirmation and keep a copy; and **revoking authorization should be free**, while a formal *stop-payment order* is a separate paid service ($25–$35 at most banks) — ask for the free revocation first and only pay if the bank says it is the only way. If a debit is scheduled in the next day or two, ask the bank point-blank whether your request stops that specific payment. Then send the lender its own written revocation (their contract almost always contains a revocation clause — use it). Never rely on the lender to stop debiting.
5. **Send a written dispute / cease-collection letter — Tier 1 and Tier 2 states only.** (Tier 3 or 4? See Part 2A; the void-loan letter does not apply to you.) Send it by email plus first-class mail at minimum, or certified mail with return receipt if you can afford the ~$9. Cite your state statute from the table, demand collection cease, revoke consent to calls and texts (TCPA, 47 U.S.C. § 227), and demand no credit reporting of the disputed debt.
6. **File all four complaints, every time:** (a) your state financial regulator; (b) your state Attorney General; (c) the **CFPB** — consumerfinance.gov/complaint or 1-855-411-2372 (the company must respond in writing in ~15 days); (d) the **FTC** — ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Free, fast, and they build the enforcement record that shuts these lenders down for everyone.
7. **Guard your credit.** Check all three bureaus free at annualcreditreport.com. If the void debt appears, dispute it in writing (FCRA, 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2). Note: most tribal/unlicensed lenders don't report to the bureaus at all — the "we'll ruin your credit" threat is usually a bluff.
8. **Know the collector rules — and put the dispute in writing.** Third-party collectors are bound by the FDCPA (15 U.S.C. § 1692). To force collection to pause, your dispute must be **in writing** and sent within **30 days of receiving their written validation notice** (15 U.S.C. § 1692g(b)) — an oral request over the phone does not trigger the pause. Email or a mailed letter both work; keep a copy. Collecting a debt that's void under state law, or threatening a suit that can't legally be filed, violates the FDCPA. Many collectors also need their own state license.
9. **Military? You have a nuclear option.** The Military Lending Act (10 U.S.C. § 987) caps loans to active-duty members and dependents at 36% MAPR nationwide; loans above it are **void from inception**. Contact your installation JAG office.
10. **Don't fall for the sequel.** "Settlement" and "consolidation" companies that cold-call you are frequently a second scam feeding on the first. Talk to a nonprofit (Part 7) before paying anyone.

## Part 2A — If You Are in a Tier 3 or Tier 4 State (read this instead of sending the void-loan letter)

If your state came up orange or red, be clear about what that means and what it does not. It means your state has no rate cap strong enough — or no clear enough remedy — to declare this loan void. **So do not send the void-loan letter in Appendix A. It cites a protection you do not have, and a lender that reads it will know immediately that you have no legal basis. Worse, if you stop paying an enforceable loan on the assumption that it is void, you can end up in genuine default: fees, collections, credit damage, and a lawsuit you would lose.** That is the one way this handbook could leave you worse off than doing nothing.

What it does **not** mean is that you have no rights. Five things are still true, and all of them are free:

1. **Your lender may still be unlicensed, and that still matters.** Nearly every state — including the weak-cap ones — requires a license to make consumer loans. A weak rate cap and no licensing requirement are different things. Run Part 5 anyway. If the lender is unlicensed, file the complaints; regulators act on exactly this, and an unlicensed lender is often more willing to negotiate than to be investigated.
2. **You can still shut off the payments.** The ACH revocation in Part 2, step 4 is federal law and works identically in all 51 jurisdictions. Stopping the automatic debit does not cancel the debt — be clear-eyed about that — but it puts you, not the lender, in control of what leaves your account.
3. **Collectors are still bound by the FDCPA** (Part 6), and you can still dispute anything false on your credit report under the FCRA.
4. **If you are active-duty military or a dependent**, the 36% Military Lending Act cap applies nationwide regardless of your state, and loans above it are void from inception. This is the one place a Tier 4 state does not matter at all.
5. **Negotiation is a real tool here, not a consolation prize.** Call the lender and ask directly for a payment plan, a hardship arrangement, or a reduced payoff. Get any agreement in writing before you pay. Nonprofit credit counseling (**nfcc.org**, 1-800-388-2227) will do this with you at no charge, and they negotiate these accounts routinely.

Then read Part 8 — the cycle-breaking part matters more for you than for anyone else, because in your state the arithmetic will not be fixed by a statute.

## Part 3 — The Tribal Lender Reality Check

Tribal lenders lead with two claims: only tribal law governs the loan, and sovereign immunity makes them untouchable. Both are narrower than they sound. Immunity makes it hard to **sue the tribal entity** — but even that has limits (the Fourth Circuit's *Williams v. Big Picture Loans* found immunity, yet the enterprise still ended in a ~half-million-borrower settlement, and its outside financier took a **$43.4 million judgment**, because immunity never protects the non-tribal partners and investors who run these schemes).

What immunity does **NOT** do: it does not make a void loan valid. Choice-of-law clauses picking tribal law over your state's protections are routinely held unenforceable as against public policy (*Gingras v. Think Finance*; *MacDonald v. CashCall*; *Hengle v. Treppa*). It does not let the lender sue you in your state and win — your state's court applies your state's void-loan statute. And in practice tribal lenders essentially **never sue borrowers**, because filing suit would expose the whole structure to a court and to counterclaims.

**The practical asymmetry:** as a *defense*, a void-loan statute is strong — but it is a defense you must raise, not a force field. Suits are rare; they are not impossible, and a lender who does sue can win if you don't show up or if the statute turns out not to cover your loan. As *offense* — clawing back what you paid — immunity makes things slower, usually requiring the non-tribal participants, an AG action, or a class case. **Escaping the debt is the reliable win; refunds are the bonus round.**

## Part 4 — The 51-Jurisdiction Map

**🟢 Tier 1:** unlicensed/over-cap loan is **void or uncollectible** — the full escape playbook applies. **🟡 Tier 2:** interest/fees void or refundable; principal may survive — you likely owe amount borrowed minus payments made, nothing more. **🟠 Tier 3:** real rate cap, weaker/unclear remedy — challengeable; get legal aid or regulator help. **🔴 Tier 4:** weak or no cap — the loan is probably legal even at an obscene rate; use the federal playbook, negotiation, and nonprofit counseling.

**The mark in front of each law:** ● = statute verified against primary sources for this edition; ◐ = classification consistent with NCLC 50-state surveys — **confirm the current citation with the regulator before acting.** The Regulator column sits third on purpose: if the rest of the table is more than you can face, that is the phone number that does the work for you, free.

**If you land in Tier 3 or Tier 4, read this before you give up.** A weak rate cap is not the same as a lawless state. Nearly every state still requires a license to make consumer loans, so an unlicensed online lender is usually breaking the law even where the penalty for it is something short of voiding the debt — your regulator complaint is real, and regulators do act on it. And every federal tool in Part 2 works identically in all 51 jurisdictions: you can still revoke the ACH authorization, still invoke the FDCPA against collectors, still dispute credit reporting, and still void the loan outright if you are active-duty military. Part 8 covers what to do about the underlying money problem.

## Part 5 — The Verification Protocol (do this BEFORE you stop paying)

Fifteen minutes of checking protects you from the one real risk in this handbook: acting on a stale entry. Laws change every year — Washington's void provision is from 2024, Minnesota's cap from 2023, and states occasionally move backward too. You do not need to be a lawyer to do this. Follow the steps mechanically:

1. **Get the law's name from the table.** In your state's row in Part 4, look at the "Key law" column. You'll see a code made of letters and numbers — for example, RCW 31.04.035 (that's Washington's) or 815 ILCS 123 (that's Illinois's). That code IS the name of the law. Copy it down exactly, symbols and all.
2. **Read the one sentence that matters.** Type the code into Google, add the word "law", and press enter. In the results, click the link that ends in **.gov** — that's your state legislature's official copy (ignore ad results and law-firm blogs). You don't need to understand the whole page. You're hunting for a single sentence about what happens to a loan made without a license or above the rate cap, and the words you want to see are **"void," "uncollectible," "unenforceable,"** or **"forfeits interest."** Found one of those words? The table was right — keep going.
3. **Confirm the lender has no license.** Go to **nmlsconsumeraccess.org** and type the lender's name in the search box — this is the national database of licensed lenders. Then go to your state regulator's website (it's in your state's row) and find the page called "Verify a License" or "License Lookup" and search there too. No results in either place means unlicensed. While you're on the regulator's site, also search the lender's name — many regulators have published warnings naming these exact companies. Screenshot everything.
4. **Confirm your loan fits — this is the step people skip, and it is the one that matters.** Finding the word "void" in a statute proves the remedy exists. It does not yet prove the remedy covers *your* loan. Five checks:
   - **Date:** you took the loan *after* the law's effective date (some protections only cover new loans).
   - **Amount:** the loan is within any dollar limit the statute names (Ohio's void provision, for example, reaches loans of $5,000 or less).
   - **Location:** you were physically in your state when you applied.
   - **The right name.** The brand on the website is often not the legal entity. Look on the loan agreement for the actual company name, and search the databases for *that*. A "no results" for a brand name proves nothing.
   - **⚠️ The bank partner.** Read your agreement for any mention of a bank — phrases like "originated by," "issued by [Bank], Member FDIC," or a bank named anywhere in the fine print. Some high-rate lenders route loans through an out-of-state bank precisely because banks aren't required to hold your state's lending license. If a bank is named, **do not assume your loan is void.** These "rent-a-bank" arrangements are legally contested and sometimes fail, but that is a fight for a lawyer, not a conclusion you can reach alone. Take it to legal aid.

   All five clear? Proceed. **Any one of them unclear or unknown? Your loan's status is UNRESOLVED — and unresolved is not the same as void.** Do not send the void-loan letter and do not stop paying on that basis yet. Go to step 5 and get it settled by someone who can settle it. You can still revoke the ACH authorization, still file complaints, and still demand written validation while it's unresolved.
5. **Unresolved, stuck, or unsure at ANY step? Don't guess — call. Guessing is the only way this handbook hurts you.** Every state regulator answers consumer questions for free, and they will run the license search for you if typing it on a phone is too much. Call the number on the Contact page of the website in your state's row and ask the two questions: *"Is [lender name] licensed to lend in this state?"* and *"What does state law say about loans made by unlicensed lenders?"* They can't give legal advice, but they answer both of those every single day. And ten minutes with a free legal aid attorney (lawhelp.org) beats months of guessing.

## Part 6 — After You Send the Letter: The Fear Campaign, the Bluffs, and the One Real Danger

The law did its job the day your letter arrived. What happens next is theater, and it follows a script so predictable you can read it in advance. Expect the calls to **spike** in the first two weeks (urgency is their only remaining tool), then thin out. Somewhere in the following months, your account may be sold to a debt buyer or handed to a collector, who restarts the script from the top. Each handoff makes your position stronger, not weaker: buyers of void debt inherit its voidness, and third-party collectors are bound by the federal FDCPA in ways the original lender wasn't. Here is the bluff decoder — what they say, and what is actually true:

- **"We're filing a lawsuit this week."** Tribal and unlicensed lenders almost never sue, because a courtroom is the one place their whole structure gets examined — the missing license, the void-loan statute, and your counterclaims with attorney's fees attached. And a collector threatening a suit it doesn't intend to file is itself violating the FDCPA (15 U.S.C. § 1692e(5)). Log the call; it's evidence.
- **"You'll be arrested" / "this is criminal fraud."** There is no debtors' prison in the United States. Failure to pay a consumer debt is never a crime, police do not call about loans, and anyone who says otherwise is lying to you — and if they're a third-party collector, breaking federal law while doing it.
- **"A process server will be at your workplace tomorrow."** Real service of process does not come with a panic-inducing warning call offering to make it all go away for a payment today. This exact line is the signature of the phantom-debt script.
- **"We'll garnish your wages / freeze your account."** Both require suing you, winning, and getting a court judgment first. No judgment, no garnishment, no levy. **One exception, and it is important:** if a loan is *secured* by your car — a title loan, or any agreement where you signed over the title — the lender can repossess it without going to court at all, under self-help repossession rules, as long as they don't breach the peace. Unsecured payday and installment loans carry no such right. Check your agreement for the word "security interest," "collateral," or "title." If your car is collateral, talk to legal aid before you stop paying anything.
- **"We'll ruin your credit."** Most of these lenders never report to the major bureaus at all, and knowingly reporting a disputed void debt exposes them under the FCRA. Check annualcreditreport.com; dispute anything that appears, attaching your letter.
- **"This is your final notice before escalation to our legal department."** You will receive several final notices. It's a rotation, not an escalation.

**The phantom-debt scammers.** After a default, loan-application data gets sold and resold, and eventually strangers call who know your Social Security number and quote your old loan back to you while threatening arrest. Three rules end every one of these calls: (1) a legitimate collector must send you a written validation notice — and to force collection to pause you must dispute **in writing** within 30 days of receiving that notice (15 U.S.C. § 1692g(b)); saying it on the phone feels satisfying but does not trigger the pause, so follow every call with a short email or letter saying "I dispute this debt and request validation," and keep a copy; (2) anyone demanding payment by gift card, wire transfer, cryptocurrency, or a payment app is a scammer, one hundred percent of the time, no exceptions; (3) never confirm your SSN, date of birth, or bank details to an inbound caller — ever. Report the call at ReportFraud.ftc.gov; phantom collection is an active FTC enforcement priority.

**What never to say or do on these calls.** Do not promise a payment. Do not make a "good-faith" partial payment on a disputed or void debt — in some states a payment restarts the statute of limitations, and it undercuts the dispute you built. Do not give a new bank account or card number. Do not accept a "settlement" on a Tier 1 void loan; you would be paying real money on a legally nonexistent debt. If any offer interests you, require it in writing first, and run it past legal aid before signing anything.

**If a debit sneaks through after your revocation.** Your bank is your backstop: under Regulation E, an electronic debit initiated after you've revoked authorization is an *unauthorized transfer*. Notify the bank in writing within 60 days of the statement showing it, attach your revocation letter, and demand an error investigation — the bank generally must resolve it within 10 business days or issue provisional credit while it investigates. This is a right against your *bank*, so it works even when the lender ignores you completely.

**How long does the lawsuit risk actually last?** Every state sets a statute of limitations — a deadline after which nobody can sue you over a debt. For consumer debts it is commonly three to six years (a few states run longer), and the clock usually starts at your last payment or your default, not the day you borrowed. Once it expires the debt is "time-barred," and filing or even threatening a lawsuit on a time-barred debt violates the FDCPA. Two consequences, both of which reinforce rules already in this handbook: (1) in many states, making even one small payment or signing a payment plan can **restart the clock** — that is the real reason never to make a "good-faith" payment on a disputed debt; and (2) time-barred status is a defense you must **raise in court yourself**, so it is not an excuse to ignore papers. Look up "[your state] statute of limitations debt" on a .gov or legal aid site. Note this is a separate clock from the seven-year credit-reporting window under the FCRA.

**⚠️ THE ONE REAL DANGER: a genuine court summons.** Everything in this handbook rests on one discipline — "void" is an affirmative defense, a shield you must *show up* to raise. The only reliable way a dead debt becomes a live wage garnishment is a **default judgment**, entered because someone believed "they can't sue me" and ignored real court papers. So: real papers carry a court caption, a case number, and a courthouse address — verify by calling the court clerk using the phone number on the court's own .gov website, never a number printed on the papers themselves. If it's fake, you've caught a scammer; report it. If it's real: respond before the deadline (typically 20–30 days), raise the void-loan statute from your state's row in Part 4 (or your State Action Kit) as your defense, attach your letter and certified-mail receipt, and call legal aid the same day. These are the cases consumer attorneys take eagerly — fee-shifting means the lender pays your lawyer — and lenders routinely dismiss rather than let a court rule their loan void on the record. Never, under any circumstances, ignore genuine court papers.

**What winning looks like.** An anticlimax. The calls spike, then thin. The debits stop. Nothing lands on your credit. Months later you notice the last contact was a form letter, and the loan hasn't ended with a certificate or an apology — it has simply ceased to exist in anyone's ledger except your folder of receipts. Keep that folder for seven years: zombie debt buyers occasionally surface years later, and one reply enclosing your dispute letter and certified-mail receipt kills the resurrection on contact. That silence is the whole victory. It's also the first payday-free paycheck you've had in a long time — spend it on yourself.

## Part 7 — Getting Your Money Back, and Getting Help

**Refund routes, in rough order of effort:** (1) **Demand it** in your cease-collection letter — in void states, payments beyond principal (in Virginia, ALL payments) are legally recoverable, and some lenders refund to make complaints disappear. (2) The **CFPB complaint** forces a written company response and sometimes shakes out a refund. (3) **Your state AG** — AGs have repeatedly forced tribal/online lenders to cancel debts and pay restitution statewide (Vermont, West Virginia, Pennsylvania, Virginia, and others). (4) A **private UDAP lawsuit** — most states award attorney's fees and many award double/treble damages, which is why consumer attorneys often take these free. (5) **Class actions** — search your lender's name + "class action settlement"; you may already be owed money.

**Free help directory**
- Legal aid by zip code: **lawhelp.org** and **lsc.gov/find-legal-aid**
- Consumer attorneys (fee-shifted, often free to you): **consumeradvocates.org/find-an-attorney** (NACA)
- Nonprofit credit counseling: **nfcc.org**, 1-800-388-2227
- The law in plain English: **nclc.org** (their 50-state surveys are the backbone of the table above)
- Servicemembers: installation JAG office; **Military OneSource** 1-800-342-9647
- Federal portals: **consumerfinance.gov/complaint** (CFPB, 1-855-411-2372) · **ReportFraud.ftc.gov** (FTC, 1-877-382-4357)

## Part 8 — Breaking the Cycle (So You Never Need This Handbook Again)

Stopping one void loan is a win. Not needing the next one is the actual escape. This part is short, and it is the part most likely to change how the next year of your life goes.

**If you have several of these loans, that is normal — and the plan is the same for each.** People in these traps commonly carry more than one, because the only way to make this week's payment was to take out the next loan. Run the whole sequence once per lender: verify the law, block that lender's debits at your bank, send that lender its own letter with its own certified-mail receipt, and file its own set of complaints. Keep one folder per lender so nothing gets confused later. If some are void and some are not — different lenders, different license status — clear the void ones first. They are free wins, and each one you kill frees up real money to deal with the legitimate debts on honest terms.

**If you are in acute crisis right now — rent, utilities, food — handle that before anything else.** Dial **211**, or go to **211.org**: a free, confidential, nationwide referral line that connects you to local rent and utility assistance, food programs, and emergency funds. Utility companies also run hardship programs and shutoff-protection rules that almost nobody thinks to ask about. A 400% loan is the most expensive possible way to solve a problem that assistance programs already exist to solve for free.

**If you truly must borrow, borrow at 28% instead of 400%.** Federal credit unions offer **Payday Alternative Loans (PALs)** under National Credit Union Administration rules: interest capped at **28%**, application fee capped at **$20**, and rollovers prohibited by regulation. PAL I runs $200–$1,000 repaid over one to six months (you generally need to have been a member about a month first). PAL II goes up to $2,000 over one to twelve months, with no waiting period after you join. Find a credit union you qualify for at **mycreditunion.gov**, join, and **ask for a PAL by name** — counter staff often will not volunteer it. Before borrowing at all, also ask about an employer paycheck advance, your utility's payment plan, and local nonprofit emergency funds.

**Build the smallest buffer you can stand.** The trap closes when an unexpected $400 shows up and there is nothing behind it. Even $10 a paycheck into a separate account — ideally at a different institution from the one your debit card draws on, so raiding it takes an extra step — eventually becomes the reason you never have to call a payday lender again. This is the least dramatic paragraph in this handbook and the one most likely to keep you out of the trap permanently.

**One tax note, so it does not ambush you.** If a lender writes off a balance, it may mail you a **Form 1099-C** for cancellation of debt, which the IRS generally treats as taxable income. Do not ignore it and do not panic: where the loan was void under your state's law, the position is that there was never an enforceable debt to forgive — but that is an argument to make with a tax professional, not alone at your kitchen table. Free help exists: **IRS VITA** free tax preparation (irs.gov/vita) and **Low Income Taxpayer Clinics** (taxpayeradvocate.irs.gov/litc), which represent people in disputes with the IRS at no charge.

## Appendix A — The Letter and the Scripts

*Everything below is self-contained — you can execute the whole plan with just this handbook. If you also have the State Action Kits companion, use your state's pre-filled letter instead of the universal one below; it has your exact statute language already written in.*

> **⚠️ The letter below is for Tier 1 and Tier 2 states only, and only after Part 5 comes back clear.** If your state is Tier 3 or Tier 4, or if Part 5 left your loan UNRESOLVED, sending it asserts a protection you may not have. Use Part 2A instead.
>
> **Sending it for $0:** email it to the lender's support address and screenshot the sent message, then mail a printed copy with an ordinary stamp. Certified mail with return receipt (~$9) is stronger proof if you can afford it — but a saved sent-email plus a mailed copy is real evidence, and sending it free beats not sending it.

**The bank call script.** Call the number on the back of your debit card and say exactly:

> "I need a stop payment on all ACH debits and any other charges from a company called [LENDER NAME], and I am revoking my authorization for them to debit my account. Please block all future debits from them."

If asked why: *"The loan is unlawful under state law and I have sent the company a written revocation."* Ask about the stop-payment fee (usually $25–35 — pay it). Write down the date, the time, and the name of the person you spoke to.

**The regulator call script.** Call the number on the Contact page of your state regulator's website (it's in your state's row in Part 4) and ask:

> "Is [LENDER NAME] licensed to lend in this state? And what does state law say about loans made by unlicensed lenders?"

**The universal letter.** Fill in every [BRACKET]. For [KEY LAW], copy the code from the "Key law" column of your state's row in Part 4. For [WHAT THE LAW SAYS], copy the consequence from that same row — for example, "a non-mortgage loan made by an unlicensed lender is null, void, uncollectable, and unenforceable." Sign it, then send it two ways the same day: certified mail with return receipt, and email.

> [DATE]
>
> [LENDER LEGAL NAME]
> Attn: Legal / Compliance Department
> [LENDER MAILING ADDRESS]
> Sent by certified mail no. [TRACKING NUMBER] and by email to [LENDER SUPPORT EMAIL]
>
> **Re: Loan No. [LOAN NUMBER] — [YOUR FULL NAME] — NOTICE OF DISPUTED UNLAWFUL LOAN AND DEMAND TO CEASE COLLECTION**
>
> To whom it may concern:
>
> This letter concerns the loan identified above, made on [LOAN DATE] in the amount of $[AMOUNT FINANCED] at a disclosed APR of [APR]%. I was a resident of, and physically located in, the State of [YOUR STATE] when this loan was applied for and made, as your records reflect.
>
> Under [KEY LAW], [WHAT THE LAW SAYS]. You hold no license from this state, and no license issued by any other body — including a tribal regulatory authority or a partner bank's charter — satisfies this state's licensing requirement or displaces its law. I dispute this debt in full.
>
> Accordingly, I demand that you and your members, managers, servicers, agents, successors, and assigns: (1) immediately cease all collection activity on this account, including electronic debits, calls, texts, emails, and letters; (2) close the account consistent with the statute cited above; (3) refund all amounts collected in violation of that statute within thirty (30) days; and (4) furnish no negative information about this account to any consumer reporting agency — any furnishing of this disputed debt will be disputed under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1681s-2, and reported to the CFPB.
>
> **Revocation of payment authorization.** Pursuant to the Electronic Fund Transfer Act and Regulation E, 12 C.F.R. § 1005.10(c), and any revocation clause in the loan agreement itself, I hereby revoke, effective three (3) business days from the date of this letter, all authorizations for you or your agents to initiate ACH debits, remotely created checks, card charges, or any other transfers from any account of mine. I have separately instructed my financial institution to block such debits; any debit initiated after the effective date is unauthorized. I further revoke, to the fullest extent permitted by law including the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, 47 U.S.C. § 227, any consent to receive calls or text messages. Communicate with me in writing only.
>
> **Regulatory complaints.** [If you have already filed them, keep this sentence: "I have filed complaints regarding this loan, with documentation, with my state financial regulator, my state Attorney General's consumer protection division, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, and the Federal Trade Commission." Never claim to have done something you have not done.] Any continued collection will be forwarded to each agency and may itself constitute an unfair or deceptive practice (see CFPB v. CashCall, Inc., No. 15-cv-7522 (C.D. Cal. 2016)) actionable under this state's consumer protection statutes.
